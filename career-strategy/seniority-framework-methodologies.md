# Seniority & Technical Career Frameworks — Methodology Landscape

> **Status:** Research baseline / framework-selection phase  
> **Purpose:** Identify established career-development and engineering-seniority models that can be adapted into an evidence-based IC career framework.

## Why this document exists

A certification or course can increase knowledge, confidence, and technical capability, but it does not automatically change a professional's market position.

The more useful question is:

> **What observable differences exist between the professional I am today and the professional I want to become?**

A strong career framework turns that question into measurable dimensions: autonomy, scope, technical depth, ownership, architecture, business impact, communication, influence, ambiguity, and so on.

The goal is therefore **not to invent a framework from scratch**. First, study established models, identify the dimensions that repeatedly distinguish Junior → Mid → Senior → Staff → Principal, and then adapt the strongest ideas into a practical framework.

## 1. Google — engineering seniority through increasing scope

Google is an important reference because progression is not simply interpreted as *"programs better"*. Increasing seniority is associated with increasing **scope, autonomy, ambiguity, impact, and influence**.

A commonly documented engineering progression is approximately:

**L3 → L4 → L5 Senior → L6 Staff → L7 Senior Staff → L8 Principal**

For this project, the particularly interesting transition is:

**L4 → L5 → L6**

This can help answer a crucial question:

> Is an experienced engineer merely technically capable, or are they already operating with Senior/Staff-level scope and behavior even if their current job title does not recognize it?

**Research note:** distinguish official Google material from employee/ex-employee reconstructions when extracting detailed behavioral criteria.

---

## 2. Microsoft — impact, scope and influence

Microsoft is another primary candidate because its engineering career allows substantial progression as an **individual contributor without requiring people management**.

The useful progression to investigate is approximately:

**Software Engineer → Senior → Principal → Partner / Distinguished-level technical roles**

The research question is not merely what titles exist, but:

> **Which dimensions actually change between those levels?**

Particular attention should be paid to impact, technical judgment, organizational scope, independence, communication, and influence.

---

## 3. IBM — technical leadership as a first-class career

IBM is particularly interesting because it has a long tradition of treating **technical leadership as a prestigious career path in its own right**, including senior technical roles such as Distinguished Engineer.

IBM's model is useful for separating three concepts that are often incorrectly collapsed into one:

**Technical expertise ≠ Architecture ≠ People management**

A senior technical contributor can provide architecture, enterprise-level technical direction, stakeholder guidance, mentoring, and business impact without becoming an administrative manager.

For this project, IBM may be especially useful for defining the upper end of an IC/architect trajectory.

---

## 4. 3Pillar — the experiential reference model

3Pillar is a particularly valuable reference because its career framework was experienced directly in practice.

The remembered structure was essentially:

> **Competency → description → required level according to seniority**

For example, conceptually:

| Competency | Junior | Mid | Senior |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Autonomy | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| Technical knowledge | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| Communication | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| Problem solving | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| Leadership | 1 | 2 | 3 |

> **Important:** The values above are illustrative, not asserted to be the actual 3Pillar matrix. The original/publicly available framework should be researched before using specific values.

The power of this model is that it makes career progression actionable. A Junior engineer can see not simply *"become Senior"*, but which specific competencies must develop and to what level.

---

## 5. PwC Professional — behaviors beyond technical expertise

PwC provides a useful counterweight to purely technical ladders.

The historical **PwC Professional** framework has included dimensions such as:

- Whole Leadership
- Business Acumen
- Technical Capabilities
- Global Acumen
- Relationships

A particularly useful idea from PwC-style career progression is evaluating not only whether someone performs at their current level, but whether their behavior already demonstrates readiness for the next one — conceptually moving through states such as:

**Below level → At level → Partially at next level → Substantially at next level**

This is powerful because promotion readiness becomes a question of **observable evidence**, not merely tenure or course completion.

PwC also prevents the future framework from becoming an overly technical checklist where seniority is equated with accumulating technologies.

---

## 6. McKinsey — separate IC and people-management tracks

McKinsey contributes less as a detailed engineering ladder and more as a **design principle**.

For advanced digital talent, career progression should allow parallel paths:

### People Leadership Track
Progress through organizational and people-management responsibility.

### Expert / Individual Contributor Track
Progress through expertise, technical judgment, scope, impact, and influence.

Crucially, senior IC roles still require capabilities such as:

- influence,
- collaboration,
- coaching,
- communication,
- business understanding.

Therefore:

> **Individual Contributor does not mean isolated technical specialist.**

An engineer can avoid people management as the core of the role while still exercising substantial leadership and organizational influence.

---

## 7. Amazon — ownership, ambiguity and delivery

Amazon is valuable because its engineering culture strongly emphasizes dimensions such as:

- ownership,
- scope,
- dealing with ambiguity,
- execution,
- delivery,
- influence.

It provides a useful comparison against Google and Microsoft: if similarly important dimensions emerge independently across organizations with very different cultures, those dimensions become stronger candidates for a general seniority model.

---

## 8. Meta — progression beyond Senior

Meta is useful because its IC ladder extends significantly beyond Senior and therefore provides evidence about what changes when **being a very good programmer is no longer sufficient to differentiate levels**.

The most interesting area for this project is the progression through approximately:

**Senior → Staff → Senior Staff → Principal-equivalent levels**

The dimensions to investigate include impact, organizational scope, technical direction, influence, and ability to multiply the effectiveness of other engineers.

# Emerging cross-framework dimensions

Before performing the detailed comparison, the following dimensions appear likely to recur across several models:

1. **Technical mastery**
2. **Autonomy**
3. **Problem complexity**
4. **Scope**
5. **Ability to operate under ambiguity**
6. **Ownership**
7. **Architecture / systems thinking**
8. **Business impact**
9. **Communication**
10. **Influence without authority**
11. **Technical leadership**
12. **Mentoring / developing others**
13. **Execution / delivery**
14. **Organizational impact**

These are **research hypotheses**, not yet the final framework.

The next stage should determine which dimensions are independently supported by multiple established methodologies.

# Proposed research method

Rather than selecting Google, PwC, IBM, or any other model prematurely:

1. **Collect** the most credible available descriptions of each framework.
2. **Extract** the dimensions used to differentiate seniority.
3. **Normalize** equivalent concepts that use different terminology.
4. **Cluster** recurring dimensions across organizations.
5. **Define** behavioral anchors for each dimension on a common 1–5 scale.
6. **Map** those levels to Senior / Staff / Principal expectations.
7. **Require evidence** before assigning a score.
8. Only then **assess the individual professional profile** against the framework.

This order matters.

The framework should be defined **before** evaluating a particular person; otherwise there is a risk of unconsciously designing an assessment around the candidate's existing strengths.

---

# Evidence instead of self-perception

A future assessment should not ask only:

> *"From 1 to 5, how autonomous do you think you are?"*

Instead, it should use professional evidence.

For example:

> **Autonomy — 4/5**  
> Receives incompletely defined problems, investigates them, designs a solution, and drives implementation without continuous supervision. Evidence must demonstrate this behavior repeatedly and in meaningful contexts. To reach 5/5, evidence might require autonomous technical direction across a broader organizational scope.

The exact behavioral anchors still need to be derived from the researched frameworks.

---

# Relationship between learning and career progression

This framework also changes how courses and certifications should be treated.

A course is **not automatically career progress**.

When considering a new learning activity, ask:

1. **Which competency does this develop?**
2. **Is there an actual gap in that competency?**
3. **What observable evidence would demonstrate movement to the next level?**
4. **Does the course produce that evidence, or merely knowledge?**

Sometimes the correct intervention will be a course.

Other times it will be:

- owning a difficult project,
- designing a system,
- resolving an ambiguous production problem,
- writing and defending an architecture decision,
- mentoring another engineer,
- delivering a product to real users,
- influencing a decision without formal authority,
- demonstrating measurable business impact.

Learning remains valuable in its own right. It simply should not be confused with evidence of increased professional seniority.

---

# Intended outcome

The eventual deliverable should be an evidence-based **IC Competency Framework**, likely containing roughly 10–15 dimensions with:

- a clear definition for every competency,
- behavioral anchors for levels 1–5,
- mapping to Senior / Staff / Principal expectations,
- evidence requirements,
- current demonstrated level,
- target level,
- identified gap,
- concrete actions capable of closing that gap.

Conceptually:

| Dimension | Current | Senior | Staff | Principal | Evidence | Gap / Action |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|---|
| Autonomy | ? | 4 | 5 | 5 | To assess | To derive |
