# Initial Go-to-Market Strategy

## Context

A recurring pattern in my professional history is that I have successfully built software for individual clients, but I have rarely attempted to sell the same solution to additional customers.

This is not primarily a technical limitation. The stronger constraint is commercial: direct selling, especially when it involves rejection, creates enough emotional friction that it can discourage consistent market outreach.

The objective is therefore **not to force a traditional salesperson behavior onto the founder**. The commercial process should be designed around strengths that already exist: understanding businesses, discovering problems, analyzing processes, and designing useful software solutions.

## Core Principle

> **Do not begin by selling. Begin by learning.**

The first interaction with a potential customer is not a sales pitch. It is a **business discovery conversation**.

The goal is to understand:

- how the business currently operates;
- where employees or owners lose time or money;
- which processes are frustrating, manual, fragile, or poorly controlled;
- what software they already use;
- what they currently pay to solve the problem;
- whether the problem is important enough that they would pay for a better solution.

This changes the psychological framing from:

**"I must convince this person to buy my product."**

into:

**"I want to discover whether there is a real match between this business problem and something we can solve."**

A conversation that does not produce a sale can therefore still be successful if it produces useful market knowledge.

## Phase 1 — Customer Discovery

The initial metric should be **conversations, not sales**.

A reasonable first experiment is approximately 10 conversations with businesses in a selected niche.

For every conversation, capture:

1. the problem described by the customer;
2. how they solve it today;
3. the cost or consequences of the problem;
4. existing software or manual workarounds;
5. willingness to change;
6. willingness to pay;
7. similarities with problems reported by other businesses.

Cold outreach is acceptable, but the request should be for a short discovery conversation rather than a product demonstration.

## Phase 2 — Look for Repetition

One customer problem is a custom project.

Repeated versions of the same problem across multiple customers may indicate a market opportunity.

Before investing heavily in a reusable product, look for evidence that:

- several businesses experience essentially the same pain;
- the pain has meaningful operational or financial consequences;
- existing alternatives are weak, expensive, obsolete, cumbersome, or poorly adapted;
- customers are already spending money or significant effort on workarounds;
- enough of the solution can be standardized to avoid rebuilding it from scratch for every customer.

The transition should therefore be:

**Custom solution → repeated problem → reusable solution → productized service/product**

rather than:

**Idea → build product → hope someone buys it.**

## Phase 3 — First Customers as Laboratories

The first one or two paying customers should be treated as learning environments as well as revenue sources.

The objective is not immediately to create a massive SaaS product. Instead:

- solve the customer's problem well;
- identify which parts are customer-specific;
- identify which parts could serve many companies;
- document implementation effort;
- measure business value;
- refine pricing and positioning;
- collect evidence that can later support commercial conversations.

The second customer is especially important: it tests whether the first solution was merely custom work or whether a repeatable business pattern actually exists.

## Commercial Role Design

A future software firm should not depend permanently on one founder performing every role.

A useful separation is:

**Technical / Product Founder**

- business discovery;
- requirements and problem analysis;
- solution architecture;
- product judgment;
- technical credibility with customers.

**Commercial Function**

- prospecting;
- follow-up;
- proposals;
- negotiation;
- closing;
- maintaining the sales pipeline.

Initially these responsibilities may overlap between founders, including a possible father-son firm. Over time, however, the company should deliberately assign the commercial function to the person who is strongest and most comfortable with it, or eventually bring in someone specifically for that role.

The business should not be designed around the assumption that the technical founder must become a conventional salesperson.

## Initial Contact Philosophy

The first contact should sound approximately like this in intent:

> We are studying how businesses like yours handle this process. I would like to understand how you currently do it, what works, and what causes problems. If we discover that something we are building could help, we can talk about that later.

This removes unnecessary pressure while preserving the possibility of a commercial opportunity.

## Success Criteria for the Initial Stage

The first stage succeeds when we can answer these questions with evidence:

- What specific business problem are we solving?
- Who experiences it repeatedly?
- How painful or expensive is it?
- How is it solved today?
- Why would someone switch?
- What would they realistically pay?
- Which parts of our solution are reusable?
- Can we find a second customer without rebuilding everything?

Only after answering these questions should significant effort go into scaling the product or building a larger sales operation.

## Guiding Idea

**The first commercial objective is not to become better at pushing software. It is to become systematic at discovering valuable problems.**

Selling becomes easier when the company has accumulated evidence that the problem is real, repeated, expensive, and worth solving.
