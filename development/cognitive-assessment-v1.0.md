# Cognitive Assessment v1.0

**Date:** 2026-09-01

## Purpose

Establish a practical cognitive baseline for evaluating readiness for high-impact senior technical roles, with emphasis on reasoning quality, problem solving, learning agility, working memory, and judgment under ambiguity.

This is **not** a clinical IQ test or a standardized psychometric instrument. The results should be interpreted as structured professional evidence, not as a population percentile or intelligence score.

## Assessment Design

The assessment was completed in three sessions totaling 65 questions.

### Session 1 — Logical, Abstract and Quantitative Reasoning

Focus areas:
- Logical deduction
- Abstract and pattern reasoning
- Quantitative reasoning
- Mixed analytical reasoning

Result: **18/20 (90%)**

Observed strengths:
- Strong pattern recognition
- Strong mixed analytical reasoning
- Good practical quantitative reasoning

Observed development areas:
- Formal implication / contraposition precision
- Percentage-change precision

### Session 2 — Verbal Reasoning, Working Memory and Rule Acquisition

Focus areas:
- Verbal reasoning
- Working memory and attention
- Learning unfamiliar rules
- Formal inference
- Higher-difficulty reasoning

Result: **18/25 (72%)**

Observed strengths:
- Very strong working memory / attention
- Strong verbal comprehension
- Good causal reasoning
- Good skepticism toward unsupported causal claims

Observed development areas:
- Necessary vs. sufficient conditions
- Converse errors in formal logic
- Initial encoding of unfamiliar rule systems

Important observation:
Some early mistakes in rule acquisition were followed by correct answers on more complex transformations. This suggests the issue is more likely initial encoding / attention than inability to learn new structures.

### Session 3 — Applied Problem Solving and Senior-Level Judgment

Focus areas:
- Production troubleshooting
- Decision-making under uncertainty
- Technical/business trade-offs
- Requirements ambiguity
- Learning an unfamiliar access-control model
- Senior engineering judgment

Result: **16/20 (80%)**

Observed strengths:
- Strong systems reasoning
- Strong technical diagnosis
- Strong risk awareness
- Strong business/technical trade-off reasoning
- Excellent performance on senior-level judgment scenarios

The final senior-level block was completed with **5/5 correct answers**.

Observed development areas:
- Tendency to seek more certainty than necessary before making a defensible decision
- Occasional over-application of restrictions in unfamiliar rule systems

## Overall Result

Raw result across all sessions:

**52/65 (~80%)**

This percentage should not be interpreted as an IQ score or percentile. The assessment was not standardized against a reference population.

## Cognitive Map

### Strong Signals

- Systems reasoning
- Abstract reasoning and pattern recognition
- Working memory
- Applied problem solving
- Causal skepticism
- Technical diagnosis
- Risk reasoning
- Business/technical trade-off judgment
- Senior engineering judgment

### Solid Signals

- Quantitative reasoning
- Verbal reasoning
- Acquisition of unfamiliar rule systems

### Development Areas

- Formal propositional logic
  - Necessary vs. sufficient conditions
  - Converse errors
  - Contraposition
- Numerical precision in percentage-change problems
- Decision-making with incomplete-but-sufficient evidence
- Avoiding unnecessary certainty-seeking before acting

## Professional Interpretation

The assessment found **no evidence of broad cognitive weakness** that would reasonably explain difficulty progressing toward more senior technical roles.

The strongest pattern is that performance improved when problems became more realistic and professionally complex. This is especially relevant because senior technical work depends less on isolated test mechanics and more on:

- Framing ambiguous problems
- Evaluating trade-offs
- Diagnosing systems
- Managing technical risk
- Distinguishing correlation from causation
- Making defensible decisions with incomplete information
- Connecting engineering decisions to business outcomes

The observed weaknesses are narrow and trainable rather than general.

## Career Implication

The evidence does **not** suggest that the primary development need is greater raw cognitive capacity, more mathematics, or another broad sequence of technical courses.

The higher-value development focus is to convert existing capability into externally visible evidence of seniority:

- Architecture decisions
- Problem framing
- Measurable business impact
- Consulting-style analysis
- Technical communication
- Product and delivery judgment
- Demonstrated ownership of complex outcomes

## Recommended Actions

1. Train formal logic precision with short exercises on implication, contraposition, and necessary/sufficient conditions.
2. Practice making explicit decisions under bounded uncertainty: identify what is known, what remains uncertain, and whether the remaining uncertainty is material enough to delay action.
3. Capture real examples of senior judgment from current and personal projects.
4. Convert those examples into portfolio evidence, CV bullets, interview stories, and technical write-ups.
5. Continue the broader assessment with the **Behavioral Profile** phase.

## Conclusion

The current evidence supports the working hypothesis that the main barrier to higher-level technical opportunities is **not cognitive capacity**.

The more actionable problem is positioning, demonstration of senior-level impact, communication, and market alignment.

This assessment should be treated as **Cognitive Assessment v1.0**, a baseline that can be revisited after future development work.
